\section{A small calculus, generated from Lean}

This paper presents a simply-typed $\lambda$-calculus whose definitions and
results are in Lean. Everything in a \texttt{lean...} environment below is
generated from the Lean development by imprimatur; the rest is written by
hand, and the hand-written statements are checked against Lean. The
examples are Lean theorems about particular terms, stated by imprimatur as
sentences; their names $\mathtt{x}$, $\mathtt{y}$, $\mathtt{z}$ and
$\mathtt{f}$ are set in typewriter, to tell them from the metavariables $x$
and $y$ that stand for any name.

\subsection{Syntax}

Terms are name-carrying: a variable is a name, and an abstraction binds a
name. The grammar is generated from the inductive types. The descriptions on
the right are the authors': imprimatur keeps them when it regenerates the
grammar, and uses the constructors' docstrings only for new ones.

\begin{leangrammar}[sum=-gvNYDo1w4, dflt=VDpvVT8fbS]{Stlc.Ty}{Types}
\label{gram:Stlc.Ty}\begin{LeanGrammar}
\LeanProduction{\tau}{types}{\mathsf{b}}{\leanslot{Stlc.Ty.base}{the base type}}
\LeanAlternative{\tau_{1} \to \tau_{2}}{\leanslot{Stlc.Ty.arrow}{functions}}
\end{LeanGrammar}
\end{leangrammar}

\begin{leangrammar}[sum=FuF0yc-Xpz, dflt=Z7b8j8i4cU]{Stlc.Tm}{Terms}
\label{gram:Stlc.Tm}\begin{LeanGrammar}
\LeanProduction{e}{terms, with names}{x}{\leanslot{Stlc.Tm.var}{a variable}}
\LeanAlternative{\lambda x{:}\tau.\, e}{\leanslot{Stlc.Tm.lam}{an abstraction, binding $x$ in $e$}}
\LeanAlternative{e_{1}\; e_{2}}{\leanslot{Stlc.Tm.app}{an application}}
\LeanAlternative{()}{\leanslot{Stlc.Tm.unit}{the unit value}}
\end{LeanGrammar}
\end{leangrammar}

Terms carry names, so two abstractions that differ only in the name they bind
are different terms; $\alpha$-equivalence, in the next section, identifies
them. A name is bound by the abstraction that carries it, and free otherwise.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=-ZmF1RzDuQ]{Stlc.ex_names}{Names matter}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_names}
\LeanProse{$(\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x}) \ne (\lambda \mathtt{y}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{y})$.}
\end{leanexample}

\begin{leanexample}[sum=58i_iI8ew9]{Stlc.ex_fv}{Bound and free names}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_fv}
\LeanProse{(1)~$\mathtt{x} \notin \mathit{fv}{(\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x}\; \mathtt{y})}$ and (2)~$\mathtt{y} \in \mathit{fv}(\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x}\; \mathtt{y})$.}
\end{leanexample}

\subsection{Names, $\alpha$-equivalence and substitution}

We handle names as Pitts does~\cite{Pitts06}. The basic operation is
\emph{swapping}: $(a\ b) \cdot e$ exchanges the names $a$ and $b$
everywhere in $e$, bound, binding and free occurrences alike. Unlike
renaming, swapping needs no side condition at a binder, and it is its own
inverse; its definition is structural:

\begin{center}
\begin{leanblock}[view=math, def, sum=kQtdL6J5XN]{Stlc.Tm.swap}{Swapping}
\LeanMath{\begin{aligned}(a\ b) \cdot x &= (a\ b)(x) \\ (a\ b) \cdot (\lambda x{:}\tau.\, e) &= \lambda (a\ b)(x){:}\tau.\, (a\ b) \cdot e \\ (a\ b) \cdot (e_{1}\; e_{2}) &= ((a\ b) \cdot e_{1})\; ((a\ b) \cdot e_{2}) \\ (a\ b) \cdot () &= ()\end{aligned}}
\end{leanblock}
\end{center}

Two abstractions are $\alpha$-equivalent when swapping each binder's name
with a name $c$ fresh for both, written $c \mathrel{\#} \ldots$, makes their
bodies $\alpha$-equivalent:

\begin{leanrules}[sum=G1XetiAjYO]{Stlc.Alpha}{Alpha-equivalence}
\begin{mathpar}

\inferrule[($\alpha$-Var)]
  { }
  { x =_{\alpha} x }

\and

\inferrule[($\alpha$-Lam)]
  {
    c \mathrel{\#} a, b, e, e' \\
    (a\ c) \cdot e =_{\alpha} (b\ c) \cdot e'
  }
  { (\lambda a{:}\tau.\, e) =_{\alpha} (\lambda b{:}\tau.\, e') }

\and

\inferrule[($\alpha$-App)]
  {
    e_{1} =_{\alpha} e_{1}' \\
    e_{2} =_{\alpha} e_{2}'
  }
  { e_{1}\; e_{2} =_{\alpha} e_{1}'\; e_{2}' }

\and

\inferrule[($\alpha$-Unit)]
  { }
  { () =_{\alpha} () }

\end{mathpar}
\end{leanrules}

The two abstractions below bind their names in opposite orders. Swapping each
binder with the fresh name $\mathtt{z}$ makes the bodies equal, so they are
$\alpha$-equivalent.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=WD7KrnrOnA]{Stlc.ex_alpha}{Two $\alpha$-equivalent terms}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_alpha}
\LeanProse{$(\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \lambda \mathtt{y}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x}) =_{\alpha} (\lambda \mathtt{y}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{y})$.}
\end{leanexample}

Substitution $e[u/x]$ is then defined by recursion on $e$. At an abstraction
$\lambda a{:}\tau.\, e$ it first swaps $a$ with a name $c$ fresh for $x$,
$a$, $e$ and $u$, so that no free name of $u$ is captured, and continues in
$(a\ c) \cdot e$\leancheck[stmt=a457f283ff65, sig=3e2ddde53b5b]{Stlc.subst}. Swapping preserves the size of a
term\leancheck[stmt=95b8589b3570, sig=c576560f0187]{Stlc.Tm.size_swap}, so the recursion terminates, although
$(a\ c) \cdot e$ is not a subterm of the abstraction. The renaming matters
when the substituted term has a free name that the binder would capture: here
$\mathtt{y}$, so the binder becomes the fresh name $\mathtt{z}$.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=RfRJkVYbYV]{Stlc.ex_subst}{Substitution avoids capture}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_subst}
\LeanProse{$(\lambda \mathtt{y}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x})[\mathtt{y}/\mathtt{x}] = (\lambda \mathtt{z}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{y})$.}
\end{leanexample}

\subsection{Typing}

The typing rules are generated from the constructors of \texttt{HasType}.
Their labels come from the constructors' docstrings. The well-formedness side
conditions of the Lean rules are left implicit, by a convention declared in
the notation module.

\begin{leanrules}[sum=nDDgEQ1-Ha]{Stlc.HasType}{Typing}
\begin{mathpar}

\inferrule[T-Var]
  {
    \Gamma(x) = \tau
  }
  { \Gamma \vdash x : \tau }

\and

\inferrule[T-Abs]
  {
    \Gamma, x{:}\tau_{1} \vdash e : \tau_{2}
  }
  { \Gamma \vdash (\lambda x{:}\tau_{1}.\, e) : \tau_{1} \to \tau_{2} }

\and

\inferrule[T-App]
  {
    \Gamma \vdash e_{1} : \tau_{1} \to \tau_{2} \\
    \Gamma \vdash e_{2} : \tau_{1}
  }
  { \Gamma \vdash e_{1}\; e_{2} : \tau_{2} }

\and

\inferrule[T-Unit]
  { }
  { \Gamma \vdash () : \mathsf{b} }

\end{mathpar}
\end{leanrules}

In the first example $\mathtt{f}$ is a function in the context, applied to the bound
variable, so every rule but T-Unit takes part in the derivation. The second
is a non-example: the unit value has only the base type, so T-App cannot
apply it, and the term has no type at all.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=9sob_x5VgU]{Stlc.ex_typing}{A typing derivation}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_typing}
\LeanProse{$\mathtt{f}{:}\mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b} \vdash (\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{f}\; \mathtt{x}) : \mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b}$.}
\end{leanexample}

\begin{leanexample}[sum=dDOa2hR2cX]{Stlc.ex_untypable}{An ill-typed term}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_untypable}
\LeanProse{$\neg (\exists \tau,\ \vdash ()\; () : \tau)$.}
\end{leanexample}

\subsection{Reduction}

Reduction is call by value. A premise that a term is a value is left
implicit: the value is written $\val$ instead. Each rule is labelled from its
constructor's name, and can be referred to: rule~\ref{rule:AppLeft}
reduces the function of an application.

\begin{leanrules}[sum=V5YXLse5tR]{Stlc.Step}{Reduction}
\begin{mathpar}

\rulelabel{Beta}{($\longrightarrow$-Beta)}%
\inferrule[($\longrightarrow$-Beta)]
  { }
  { (\lambda x{:}\tau.\, e)\; \val \longrightarrow e[\val/x] }

\and

\rulelabel{AppLeft}{($\longrightarrow$-App Left)}%
\inferrule[($\longrightarrow$-App Left)]
  {
    e_{1} \longrightarrow e_{1}'
  }
  { e_{1}\; e_{2} \longrightarrow e_{1}'\; e_{2} }

\and

\rulelabel{AppRight}{($\longrightarrow$-App Right)}%
\inferrule[($\longrightarrow$-App Right)]
  {
    e_{2} \longrightarrow e_{2}'
  }
  { \val\; e_{2} \longrightarrow \val\; e_{2}' }

\end{mathpar}
\end{leanrules}

The identity applied to the unit value $\beta$-reduces at once. When the
argument is itself an application, rule~\ref{rule:AppRight} reduces it first:
the outer $\beta$-reduction waits until the argument is a value.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=iLycR2llh0]{Stlc.ex_beta}{A $\beta$-reduction}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_beta}
\LeanProse{$(\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x})\; () \longrightarrow ()$.}
\end{leanexample}

\begin{leanexample}[sum=Au9X2TsAWI]{Stlc.ex_cbv}{Call by value}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_cbv}
\LeanProse{$(\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x})\; ((\lambda \mathtt{y}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{y})\; ()) \longrightarrow (\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x})\; ()$.}
\end{leanexample}

\subsection{Results}

A result can be generated, as a sentence; its key can be a title, declared
in the notation module:

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=zzIi1wbp85]{Swapping is involutive}{Swapping is involutive}
\label{thm:Swapping-is-involutive}
\LeanProse{Let $a, b \in \mathit{Name}$ and $e \in \mathit{Term}$. Then $(a\ b) \cdot ((a\ b) \cdot e) = e$.}
\end{leantheorem}

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=Llwby7f-kQ]{Values are final}{Values are final}
\label{thm:Values-are-final}
\LeanProse{Let $\val \in \mathit{Val}$ and $e \in \mathit{Term}$. Then $\neg (\val \longrightarrow e)$.}
\end{leantheorem}

Or as a rule, from a theorem whose hypotheses become its premises:
\begin{center}
\begin{leanblock}[view=math, rule, sum=fpyxtM_nmj]{Stlc.step_app}{Congruence}
\LeanMath{\inferrule{e_{1} \longrightarrow e_{1}'}{e_{1}\; e_{2} \longrightarrow e_{1}'\; e_{2}}}
\end{leanblock}
\end{center}

Or the prose can be written by hand and checked: the unit value has only
the base type, that is, if $\Gamma \vdash () : \tau$ then
$\tau = \mathsf{b}$\leancheck[stmt=a39c03ca314b, sig=59daca2b7dd5]{Stlc.unit_type}. Should the Lean statement of
\verb|unit_type| change, \texttt{imprimatur check} reports this sentence.

Instances of these results are examples too. Swapping renames the binder
along with the free occurrence, and swapping again
(Theorem~\ref{thm:Swapping-is-involutive}) gives the term back. The unit
value's only type is $\mathsf{b}$, so it has no function type.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=Kp3J3ralk_]{Stlc.ex_swap}{Swapping a binder}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_swap}
\LeanProse{$(\mathtt{x}\ \mathtt{y}) \cdot (\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{y}) = (\lambda \mathtt{y}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x})$.}
\end{leanexample}

\begin{leanexample}[sum=vrAVDD3vBM]{Stlc.ex_unit_not_arrow}{The unit value is not a function}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_unit_not_arrow}
\LeanProse{$\neg (\vdash () : \mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b})$.}
\end{leanexample}

\subsection{Type safety}

The substitution lemma is proved as Pitts's paper suggests. Typing is
equivariant: swapping two names throughout a term and its context preserves
its type.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=T-GyIH9gSH]{Equivariance}{Equivariance}
\label{thm:Equivariance}
\LeanProse{Let $a, b \in \mathit{Name}$, $\Gamma \in \mathit{Context}$, $e \in \mathit{Term}$ and $\tau \in \mathit{Type}$. Assume $\Gamma \vdash e : \tau$. Then $(a\ b) \cdot \Gamma \vdash (a\ b) \cdot e : \tau$.}
\end{leantheorem}

Applied to Example~\ref{ex:Stlc.ex_typing}, swapping $\mathtt{f}$ and
$\mathtt{x}$ throughout the context and the term, the function is now called
$\mathtt{x}$ and the bound variable $\mathtt{f}$.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=Ab7S8d4Yhx]{Stlc.ex_equivariance}{Equivariance at work}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_equivariance}
\LeanProse{$\mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b} \vdash (\lambda \mathtt{f}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x}\; \mathtt{f}) : \mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b}$.}
\end{leanexample}

Typing also depends on the context only at the term's free names, which
gives weakening and shadowing at once\leancheck[stmt=8f91408eb45c, sig=a65d051214fc]{Stlc.HasType.agree}. So
substitution preserves types: if $\Gamma, x{:}\tau_1 \vdash e : \tau$ and
$\Gamma \vdash u : \tau_1$, then $\Gamma \vdash e[u/x] : \tau$. At an
abstraction, substitution renames the binder to a fresh name by swapping, and
equivariance carries the typing of its body across the renaming; the
induction is on the size of $e$, which swapping
preserves\leancheck[stmt=6842915c5069, sig=105ed38d10fd]{Stlc.subst_typing}. Type safety follows.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=VQLUk38uFC]{Preservation}{Preservation}
\label{thm:Preservation}
\LeanProse{Let $e, e' \in \mathit{Term}$, $\Gamma \in \mathit{Context}$ and $\tau \in \mathit{Type}$. Assume $e \longrightarrow e'$ and $\Gamma \vdash e : \tau$. Then $\Gamma \vdash e' : \tau$.}
\end{leantheorem}

A closed, well-typed application of a function to the identity reduces by
rule~\ref{rule:Beta}, and the result has the same type.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=LuQF7rf3rY]{Stlc.ex_preservation}{A step preserves the type}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_preservation}
\LeanProse{(1)~$\vdash (\lambda \mathtt{f}{:}\mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{f}\; ())\; (\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x}) : \mathsf{b}$, (2)~$(\lambda \mathtt{f}{:}\mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{f}\; ())\; (\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x}) \longrightarrow (\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x})\; ()$ and (3)~$\vdash (\lambda \mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b}.\, \mathtt{x})\; () : \mathsf{b}$.}
\end{leanexample}

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=CxgvvaS6H8]{Progress}{Progress}
\label{thm:Progress}
\LeanProse{Let $e \in \mathit{Term}$ and $\tau \in \mathit{Type}$. Assume $\vdash e : \tau$. Then $e \in \mathit{Val} \vee (\exists e',\ e \longrightarrow e')$.}
\end{leantheorem}

Progress needs the term to be closed. An open term can be well typed and yet
stuck: neither a value nor able to reduce, since no rule applies to a
variable.

\begin{leanexample}[sum=atC6biB2FK]{Stlc.ex_stuck}{An open term may be stuck}
\label{ex:Stlc.ex_stuck}
\LeanProse{(1)~$\mathtt{x}{:}\mathsf{b} \to \mathsf{b} \vdash \mathtt{x}\; () : \mathsf{b}$, (2)~$\mathtt{x}\; () \notin \mathit{Val}$ and (3)~$\forall e : \mathit{Term},\ \neg (\mathtt{x}\; () \longrightarrow e)$.}
\end{leanexample}

What the archive claims
On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge announced a proof of the Riemann
Hypothesis found with Claude, and released it as Lean alone: a four-page note1
and an archive of about 175 000 Lean files, with no human-readable argument.
The question everyone asked first was not whether the proof is right but what,
exactly, the Lean proves. This note states the five theorems the archive claims,
in the words of their Lean statements. Every claim below is generated by
imprimatur from a Lean file that imports Mathlib and states the five theorems
with sorry for every proof: the file says what is claimed, and proves nothing.
The archive is not here, and nothing in this note says that its proofs are correct.
The two halves of the question are checked by two tools. Lean’s Comparator
(lake comparator) checks that the archive proves exactly the statements of
a challenge file like the one behind this note.2
imprimatur checks that this
note says exactly what that file says: every claim is rendered from its Lean
statement, and every hand-written sentence tied to Lean with \leancheck is
checked against the statement it describes. Neither tool reads the proof.
1 The Riemann Hypothesis
Mathlib defines the Riemann Hypothesis as a proposition, and the archive’s
headline theorem proves that proposition. The claim is stated here with the
definition unfolded: a zero of ζ that is not one of the trivial zeros, and is not
the point 1, has real part one half.
Claim 1 (The Riemann Hypothesis). Let s ∈ C. Assume ζ(s) = 0, s ̸=
−2(n + 1) for every n ∈ N and s ̸= 1. Then ℜs = 1
2 .
Two clauses deserve a word. The trivial zeros are the negative even integers,
where ζ vanishes for reasons that have nothing to do with the hypothesis. The
clause s ̸= 1 is there because Mathlib’s riemannZeta is a function on all of C:
at the pole s = 1 it takes an arbitrary value, so a statement about its zeros
must set that point aside. The same claim, written with its hypotheses as an
implication rather than inside Mathlib’s definition, reads the same:
Claim 2 (The Riemann Hypothesis, restated). Let s ∈ C. Assume ζ(s) = 0,
s ̸= −2(n + 1) for every n ∈ N and s ̸= 1. Then ℜs = 1
2 .

2 Dirichlet L-functions
The archive extends the method to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. A
Dirichlet character modulo N is a homomorphism from the units of Z/N to C,
extended by zero, and its L-function L(χ, s) generalises ζ, which is L(1, s) for
the trivial character modulo 1. The first claim is that an L-function has no
zero to the right of the critical line. Its one exception is the trivial character
at s = 1, where L(1, s) is ζ(s) and has its pole, which Mathlib again gives an
arbitrary value.
Claim 3 (No zeros right of the line). Let N ∈ N, χ ∈ Char(N) and s ∈ C.
Assume 1
2 < ℜs and χ ̸= 1 ∨ s ̸= 1. Then L(χ, s) ̸= 0.
The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis itself is the next claim: every zero of
an L-function in the critical strip lies on the critical line. Mathlib’s characters
are indexed by a modulus N ∈ N that must not be zero, a hypothesis Lean
keeps in an instance binder and the claim states in words.
Claim 4 (The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis). Let N ∈ N, χ ∈ Char(N)
and s ∈ C. Assume 0 < ℜs, ℜs < 1 and L(χ, s) = 0. Then ℜs = 1
2 .
A character is primitive when it is not induced from a character of a smaller
modulus. The last claim is the hypothesis for primitive characters, which implies
the general one, since the L-function of an imprimitive character differs from
that of the primitive character inducing it by finitely many Euler factors, none
of which vanishes in the strip.
Claim 5 (The hypothesis for primitive characters). Let N ∈ N, χ ∈ Char(N)
and s ∈ C. Assume χ is primitive, 0 < ℜs, ℜs < 1 and L(χ, s) = 0. Then
ℜs = 1
2 .

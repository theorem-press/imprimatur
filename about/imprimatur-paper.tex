\title{imprimatur: papers that say what Lean proved}
\author{The imprimatur authors}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
A paper about a Lean development restates its definitions and theorems in
mathematical notation. imprimatur generates those restatements from the
development, keeps the text the authors wrote around them, and checks that
the paper still says what Lean proved. Between the development and the
paper there is one file, the bundle, and we give it a type and a meaning:
what it says of each theorem and definition, and when that is true of the
development. We then state what a successful \texttt{imprimatur check}
guarantees, in two stages: first for the mathematics the paper generates,
then for the prose its authors write and attest; and we prove it in Lean,
as one theorem: a paper that checks clean against a true bundle is true. We
prove it first for a model of imprimatur's splicer, and then for the
splicer's own code: the functions the \texttt{imprimatur} binary runs. This paper is itself built with
imprimatur: every displayed definition and theorem below is generated from
that Lean development, and every hand-written claim about it is checked
against it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A paper and its Lean development drift apart. A theorem is weakened in Lean
and not in the paper; a definition gains a case; a lemma the paper cites is
proved with \texttt{sorry}. imprimatur closes the gap in two ways. Blocks of
the paper are \emph{generated}: the extractor renders a Lean constant's
statement in the paper's notation, and the splicer writes it into the paper,
keeping any text the authors wrote in its \emph{slots}. Hand-written prose
about a constant is \emph{attested}: a \verb|\leancheck| beside it records
the hash of the statement the prose was checked against, and the check
fails if the statement changes. Knuth's \textsc{web} generates a program and its
documentation from the same source, so that the two are consistent with
each other~\cite{Knuth84}.

imprimatur is two programs. The extractor runs inside Lean, at the
development's own toolchain, and reads its elaborated environment. The
splicer edits \TeX{} and uses nothing of Lean but its standard library, so
one splicer serves every toolchain. All that passes between them is the
bundle, a JSON file. So there are three things, and two relations between
them:
\begin{center}
  the development $W$
  $\xrightarrow{\;\text{the bundle is true of it}\;}$
  the bundle $b$
  $\xrightarrow{\;\text{the paper checks clean}\;}$
  the paper $d$.
\end{center}
What does it mean for the paper to be correct? That what it shows is what
Lean proved. We say this without mentioning the bundle (what a paper claims
of a development, Section~\ref{sec:check}), say what makes a bundle true of
a development (Section~\ref{sec:meaning}), and prove that the two relations
compose: \emph{a paper that checks clean against a true bundle is true}. The
proof is first for a model of the splicer, in which a document is already
parsed into pieces, and then, in Section~\ref{sec:code}, for the splicer's
own code, which the model abstracts. It comes in two stages. The generated mathematics needs only
the renderings in the bundle. The attested prose needs the hashes, and we
say why. A second theorem, about the notation, says that a rendering can be
read back as the statement it came from; imprimatur checks it for each
formula it renders (Section~\ref{sec:faithful}).

\subsection{The trusted base}\label{sec:trusted}

What we prove is this: if the bundle is true of the development, and the
function \verb|imprimatur check| runs reports nothing, warnings included,
then every claim the \TeX{} source of the paper makes is true of the
development\leancheck[stmt=5d019f9d2388, sig=be0c5901b286]{SpliceModel.Theorem1.checkDoc_sound}. The proof is of the code that runs (Section~\ref{sec:code}), and rests on Lean's
standard axioms only. What it does not cover is trusted, and falls into
five parts.

\paragraph{Lean.} Its kernel, which checks the proofs; and its compiler,
which must compute what the definitions say. The splicer's verified code
replaces none of its definitions by unverified code: where a loop runs
tail-recursively, a theorem says it computes the same as the definition it
replaces.

\paragraph{The hypothesis.} That the bundle is true of the development is
the extractor's claim. The model says what it means, and that an honest
extractor's bundle is true (Theorem~\ref{thm:The-bundle-of-a-world-is-true});
the extractor itself, which renders and hashes inside Lean, is
metaprogramming and is not verified.

\paragraph{Reading the paper.} The theorems are about what the splicer
reads, so how it reads is trusted:
\begin{itemize}
\item The splicer's shell, which reads the files a document reaches, the
  configuration and the bundle, and writes what \verb|apply| returns. The
  theorems are about the functions it calls, given what it read.
\item The scanner's reading of \TeX{}: what is a comment, \verb|\verb|, a
  verbatim environment or a conditional branch that is off. The scanner is
  proved lossless, and the theorems make it the specification of where a
  paper's markup is; they cannot make it agree with \TeX{}'s category codes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Between the check and the reader.} The theorems stop at \TeX{}
source. What the reader sees depends further on:
\begin{itemize}
\item Normalisation. A region is the rendering after line endings,
  trailing blanks and blank lines at its ends are normalised; that these
  do not change what \TeX{} typesets is assumed.
\item The parser that reads formulas back, and, where an expected reading
  is incomplete, the grammar's mirroring of the renderer.
\item The notation module, as the paper's dictionary. Lean checks its names,
  not its choice of symbols: a rule that wrote addition as $a - b$ would read
  back faithfully. Appendix~\ref{app:notation} puts the dictionary in the
  paper, where a reader can check it.
\item What the notation declares invisible. A hidden premise makes the
  theorem shown stronger than the one proved, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:Values-are-final}, whose
  binder does not say that $V$ is a value; a synonym is an equation the
  notation asserts and nothing checks.
\item The text of a block that is not a formula: the authors' text in
  slots, and the sentences of the \verb|prose| view (\emph{Let \ldots\
  Assume \ldots\ Then \ldots}), whose reading the view fixes.
\item \TeX{} itself, in which two macros may print alike.
\end{itemize}
Wiedijk calls a failure of this kind \emph{weak}: one that needs
definitions or notation beyond a system's basic library~\cite{Wiedijk12}.
The notation's entries above are all weak, since imprimatur's printer is
the notation module. What translation validation rules out
(Section~\ref{sec:faithful}) is the rest: a rendering that reads back, in
the notation's own grammar, as a statement other than its own.

\paragraph{Attested prose.} A \verb|\leancheck| records the first 12
hexadecimal digits of a statement hash. That the prose still faces the
statement it was checked against therefore assumes that no two statements
in question share those 48 bits: safe against accident, but not against a
forger. And the prose is only as right as the person who checked it.

\section{The bundle}\label{sec:bundle}

An entry is what the bundle says of a key: what the key names, its
statement, and its proof. The statement and the proof are not in the file,
and could not be, since the splicer cannot read Lean. The file holds their
images. So an entry is parametrised by how the two are represented:

\begin{leangrammar}[sum=y7VOO7qd76, dflt=UXRbK7fNW3]{Imprimatur.Entry}{Entries}
\label{gram:Imprimatur.Entry}\begin{LeanDisplay}
\LeanCategory{\texttt{Entry}(\sigma, \pi)}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Entry}{An entry: what a bundle says of a key}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{decl} : \texttt{Decl}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Entry.decl}{what the key names}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{statement} : \sigma}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Entry.statement}{its statement}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{verdict} : \pi}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Entry.verdict}{the verdict on its proof}}
\end{LeanDisplay}
\end{leangrammar}

Of the statement, the file holds two images. The hash \emph{names} it: two
statements with one hash are one statement, as far as a document can tell.
The views \emph{show} it: each is the statement rendered by the paper's
notation. The \TeX{} is a parameter too, $\tau$: in the file it is \TeX{};
in the model it is \TeX{} with the slots already parsed, a body
(Section~\ref{sec:documents}). The names it uses are strings:

\begin{leandef}[sum=nilI_jgzRM, dflt=lRpPyy-xAJ]{Names}{Names}
\begin{LeanDisplay}
\LeanClause{k \in \mathit{Key} \;\deq\; \mathit{String}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Key}{A key: what a document cites, the name of a constant or the title of a group}}\\
\LeanClause{v \in \mathit{View} \;\deq\; \mathit{String}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.View}{The name of a view: \LeanCodeInline{math}, \LeanCodeInline{prose}, \LeanCodeInline{rules}, \LeanCodeInline{math|def=}, …}}\\
\LeanClause{\mathit{TeX} \;\deq\; \mathit{String}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.TeX}{TeX source}}\\
\LeanClause{h \in \mathit{Hash} \;\deq\; \mathit{String}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Hash}{A SHA-256, as 64 hexadecimal digits}}
\end{LeanDisplay}
\end{leandef}

\begin{leangrammar}[sum=1FePWA7SoM, dflt=IR-OTMnjJo]{Imprimatur.Image}{Images of a statement}
\label{gram:Imprimatur.Image}\begin{LeanDisplay}
\LeanCategory{\texttt{Image}(\tau)}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Image}{A statement as a document may carry it}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{stmtHash} : \mathsf{Option}(\mathit{Hash})}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Image.stmtHash}{the hash of the statement's meaning: the statement, with every definition it depends on; none when the dependencies could not be followed}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{sigHash} : \mathsf{Option}(\mathit{Hash})}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Image.sigHash}{the hash of the statement as written, the \LeanCodeInline{lean} view; none for a title}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{views} : \mathsf{Array}(\mathit{View} \times \tau)}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Image.views}{the renderings, by view}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{errors} : \mathsf{Array}(\mathit{View} \times \mathit{String})}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Image.errors}{the views that could not be rendered, and why}}\\

\LeanEntry{\mathit{macros} : \mathsf{Array}(\mathit{String})}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Image.macros}{the TeX macros the renderings use}}
\end{LeanDisplay}
\end{leangrammar}

Of the proof, the file holds only a verdict: whether Lean accepted it, and

\section{What a bundle means}\label{sec:meaning}

A bundle is about an environment, which the splicer never sees. A
\emph{world} is the environment as the extractor presents it: what each key
states and whether its proof is trusted; how a statement is hashed and
rendered; and when a rendering reads back as a statement, up to what the
notation makes invisible (Section~\ref{sec:faithful}).

\begin{leangrammar}[sum=fsA8oFAYhv, dflt=ZXPQgExx8a]{Imprimatur.World}{Worlds}
\label{gram:Imprimatur.World}\begin{LeanDisplay}
\LeanCategory{W \in \texttt{World}(\tau)}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World}{An environment, as the extractor presents it}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{Stmt} : \mathrm{Type}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.Stmt}{statements}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{kind} : \mathit{Key} \to \texttt{Kind}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.kind}{what a key names}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{stmt} : \mathit{Key} \to \mathsf{Option}(\mathit{Stmt}_{W})}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.stmt}{what a key states, if it is declared}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{trusted} : \mathit{Key} \to \mathbb{B}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.trusted}{whether a key's proof is trusted}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{hash} : \mathit{Stmt}_{W} \to \mathit{Hash}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.hash}{the hash of a statement's meaning}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{sig} : \mathit{Stmt}_{W} \to \mathit{Hash}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.sig}{the hash of a statement as written}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{render} : \mathit{View} \to \mathit{Stmt}_{W} \to \mathsf{Option}(\tau)}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.render}{a statement rendered in a view, when the view applies}}\\
\LeanEntry{\mathit{Reads} : \tau \to \mathit{Stmt}_{W} \to \mathrm{Prop}}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.World.Reads}{when a rendering reads back as a statement}}
\end{LeanDisplay}
\end{leangrammar}

A statement is what a key names, as Lean holds it: for a theorem, its
proposition; for a definition, its type and its definition; for an
inductive type or predicate, its type and its constructors; and for the
title of a group, its members' statements together. The model looks no
further. $\mathit{Stmt}_{W}$ is abstract, and a statement is known only by
what the world says of it: its two hashes, its renderings, and which
renderings read back as it. The two hashes divide it. $\mathit{sig}_{W}$
is of the statement as written, and changes when it is reworded;
$\mathit{hash}_{W}$ is of what it means, the statement with every
definition it depends on, and changes when any of them does.

For the example, $\mathit{stmt}_{W}$ at the key of \emph{Values are
final} is the proposition $\neg (V \longrightarrow M)$ under its binders;
$\mathit{kind}_{W}$ says theorem; $\mathit{trusted}_{W}$ is true;
$\mathit{hash}_{W}$ of the proposition covers $\mathit{Step}$,
$\mathit{Val}$ and $\mathit{Term}$, and substitution through (Beta);
$\mathit{sig}_{W}$ is the hash of the source shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:example}; $\mathit{render}_{W}$ in the \texttt{prose} view is the
sentence shown there, and in the \texttt{grammar} view is none, a theorem
having no grammar; and $\mathit{reads}_{W}$ holds of the sentence's
formula, which parses back uniquely in the notation of
Appendix~\ref{app:notation}.

A world has statements and verdicts but no proofs. An image is a function
of the statement alone, so nothing in a bundle can depend on how a theorem
was proved, only on whether it was. An image is \emph{of} a statement when
every claim it makes is true of that statement:

\begin{leanrules}[sum=He57VsxTNN]{Imprimatur.Image.Of}{Images of a statement}
\begin{mathpar}

\inferrule[Image]
  {
    \forall h : \mathit{Hash},\ i.\mathit{stmtHash} = h \implies h = \mathit{hash}_{W}(S) \\
    \forall h : \mathit{Hash},\ i.\mathit{sigHash} = h \implies h = \mathit{sig}_{W}(S) \\
    \forall v : \mathit{View},\ \forall t : \tau,\ (v, t) \in i.\mathit{views} \implies \mathit{render}_{W}(v, S) = t \\
    \forall v : \mathit{View},\ \forall t : \tau,\ (v, t) \in i.\mathit{views} \implies \mathit{problems}_{i}(v) = [] \implies \mathit{reads}_{W}(t, S)
  }
  { \mathit{image}_{W}(S, i) }

\end{mathpar}
\end{leanrules}

An image may say less than the environment knows: a hash it could not
compute, a view it could not render. It may never say something false. Where
it is silent, \verb|check| reports the silence (a missing view, a missing
hash) rather than trusting it. An entry is true of a key when the key names
what it says, its image is of the key's statement, and its verdict, read as
a bit, is the world's; a bundle is true when the entry it gives for each key
is.

\begin{leanrules}[sum=SBbLnxo-_9]{Imprimatur.Entry.True}{True entries}
\begin{mathpar}

\inferrule[Entry]
  {
    e.\mathit{decl}.\mathit{kind} = \mathit{kind}_{W}(k) \\
    \exists S,\ \mathit{stmt}_{W}(k) = S \wedge \mathit{image}_{W}(S, e.\mathit{statement}) \\
    \mathit{trusted}(e.\mathit{verdict}) = \mathit{trusted}_{W}(k)
  }
  { W \models_{k} e }

\end{mathpar}
\end{leanrules}

\begin{leandef}[sum=BO-FFbUkKC, dflt=ajF32tA2Kd]{Imprimatur.Bundle.True}{True bundles}
\begin{LeanDisplay}
\LeanClause{W \models b \;\deq\; \forall k : \mathit{Key},\ \forall e : \texttt{Entry}(\texttt{Image}(\tau), \pi),\ b[k] = e \implies W \models_{k} e}{\leanslot{Imprimatur.Bundle.True}{A bundle is true of a world when the entry it gives for each key is}}
\end{LeanDisplay}
\end{leandef}

One thing follows at once, for the rendering: the one an entry has for a
view is the key's statement rendered, and reads back as it when the view
has no problems.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=Xa6Hcbi0zI]{A view shows the statement}{A view shows the statement}
\label{thm:A-view-shows-the-statement}
\LeanProse{Let $\tau, \pi$ be types, $W \in \texttt{World}(\tau)$, $\mathit{trusted} \in \pi \to \mathbb{B}$, $b \in \texttt{Bundle}(\texttt{Image}(\tau), \pi)$, $k \in \mathit{Key}$, $e \in \texttt{Entry}(\texttt{Image}(\tau), \pi)$, $v \in \mathit{View}$ and $t \in \tau$. Assume $W \models b$, $b[k] = e$ and $\mathit{view}_{v}(e.\mathit{statement}) = t$. \section{The splicer's laws}

Four laws make \verb|apply| safe to run on a paper the authors are editing.
First, it changes nothing they wrote outside generated blocks. Write
$\lfloor d \rfloor$ for the outline of document $d$: its text, its checks,
and its blocks without their bodies.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=3p5XLvWAci]{Locality}{Locality}
\label{thm:Locality}
\LeanProse{Let $b \in \mathit{Bundle}$ and $d \in \mathit{Doc}$. Then $\lfloor \mathsf{apply}_{b}(d) \rfloor = \lfloor d \rfloor$.}
\end{leantheorem}

Second, running it again changes nothing more. This needs each rendering's
slots to be distinct, which we write $\mathsf{wf}(b)$\leancheck[stmt=c6662354836a, sig=9d550ab02488]{SpliceModel.WF}.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=rUqQQUgoM3]{Idempotence}{Idempotence}
\label{thm:Idempotence}
\LeanProse{Let $b \in \mathit{Bundle}$ and $d \in \mathit{Doc}$. Assume $\mathsf{wf}(b)$. Then $\mathsf{apply}_{b}(\mathsf{apply}_{b}(d)) = \mathsf{apply}_{b}(d)$.}
\end{leantheorem}

Third, \verb|check| agrees with \verb|apply|: after it, no block is stale.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=ajKQ-ezoOC]{Fixed point}{Fixed point}
\label{thm:Fixed-point}
\LeanProse{Let $b \in \mathit{Bundle}$, $d \in \mathit{Doc}$ and $k \in \mathit{Key}$. Assume $\mathsf{wf}(b)$. Then $\mathsf{stale}(k) \notin \mathsf{check}_{b}(\mathsf{apply}_{b}(d))$.}
\end{leantheorem}

Fourth, the text the authors wrote in a slot survives regeneration, as long
as the rendering still has that slot.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=4WbuqUPxi1]{Slot preservation}{Slot preservation}
\label{thm:Slot-preservation}
\LeanProse{Let $i \in \mathit{SlotId}$, $t \in \mathit{String}$, $\mathit{old} \in \mathsf{List}(\mathit{SlotId} \times \mathit{String})$ and $r \in \mathit{Body}$. Assume $i \in \mathsf{ids}(r)$ and $\mathit{old}[i] = t$. Then $\mathsf{slots}(\mathsf{fill}(\mathit{old}, r))[i] = t$.}
\end{leantheorem}


\section{What \texttt{check} guarantees}
\label{sec:check}

What does a paper claim? We say it of the world alone, with no bundle in
sight, and in two stages: first for the mathematics the paper generates,
then, in Section~\ref{sec:attested}, for the prose its authors write.

A block $\mathsf{block}_{k}^{v}(r)$ claims that the key $k$ states something
Lean proved, that $r$ is that statement rendered in view $v$, with the
authors' text in its slots, and that the rendering reads back as the
statement\leancheck[stmt=bdd129291fef, sig=a6e738ff2142]{SpliceModel.Piece.True}. Nothing here involves a hash:
the splicer compares the block's text with the rendering in the bundle,
and the bundle's verdict says the proof is trusted. If \verb|check| reports
nothing against a true bundle, warnings included, every block's claim
holds.

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=g6Fxb8r-Rw]{Generated mathematics}{Generated mathematics}
\label{thm:Generated-mathematics}
\LeanProse{Let $W \in \mathit{World}$, $b \in \mathit{Bundle}$, $d \in \mathit{Doc}$, $k \in \mathit{Key}$, $v \in \mathit{View}$ and $r \in \mathit{Body}$. Assume $W \models b$, $\mathsf{check}_{b}(d) = []$ and $\mathsf{block}_{k}^{v}(r) \in d$. Then $W \models \mathsf{block}_{k}^{v}(r)$.}
\end{leantheorem}

\paragraph{The example.} Apply the theorem to the block of \emph{Values
are final} in Figure~\ref{fig:example}. Here $W$ is the example's world;
$b$ is the bundle this paper was built with, which is true of $W$ and
against which \verb|check| reports nothing; $d$ is this paper; $k$ is the
key ``\texttt{Values are final}''; $v$ is ``\texttt{prose}''; and $r$ is the
sentence ``Let $V, M \in \mathit{Term}$. Then $\neg (V \longrightarrow
M)$.'' The conclusion, $W \models \mathsf{block}^{\mathtt{prose}}_{k}(r)$,
unfolds into five facts:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathit{stmt}_{W}(k) = S$, for some statement $S$. The key names a
  declaration, \texttt{Stlc.values\_final} at line 54 of
  \texttt{Stlc.lean}, and $S$ is its proposition, the one of
  Section~\ref{sec:meaning}: for all terms $v$ and $e$, if $v$ is a value
  then $v$ does not step to $e$.
\item $\mathit{trusted}_{W}(k) = \mathsf{true}$. Lean accepted a proof of
  $S$ that uses no \texttt{sorry} and no axiom, and none of the sixteen
  constants in its cone is untrusted. The bundle's verdict records just
  this (\texttt{"axioms": []}, \texttt{"sorry": false}, a cone of size 16
  with nothing untrusted), and $W \models b$ makes the record true.
\item $\mathit{render}_{W}(\mathtt{prose}, S) = r'$. The \texttt{prose}
  view of $S$ is a sentence $r'$: the statement's binders become
  \emph{Let \ldots} and its conclusion \emph{Then \ldots}.
\item $r = \mathsf{fill}(\mathsf{slots}(r), r')$. The sentence on the page
  is that rendering, with the authors' text in its slots. The
  \texttt{prose} view has no slots, so $r$ is $r'$ itself. For the terms
  grammar above it, the same fact says more: the formulas are the
  rendering, and the descriptions are the authors'.
\item $\mathit{reads}_{W}(r', S)$. The formula $\neg (V \longrightarrow
  M)$ parses back, in the notation of Appendix~\ref{app:notation}, to $S$
  and to nothing else. There $\longrightarrow$ is reduction,
  \texttt{Stlc.Step}, and $M$ ranges over terms. $V$ ranges over values,
  by the notation's convention that values are written $V$, which leaves
  the source's premise \texttt{IsValue v} implicit.
\end{itemize}
So a reader who trusts the extractor and the notation may conclude:
\emph{what I read is a theorem of this development, and Lean proved it.}

What the reader may not conclude is that the theorem says what they would
like: $V = V$ would pass as readily. Nor that substitution is real: the
sentence does not mention it, so the block is the rendering of a proved
statement in the world with the stand-in and in the world without, and
nothing in Theorem~\ref{thm:Generated-mathematics} tells the two apart.
That is the hash's job.

\subsection{What two views show}\label{sec:views}

Theorem~\ref{thm:Generated-mathematics} holds of every view alike, and
says the same of each: the block is the rendering, and the rendering reads
back. It can say no more, because a world says nothing of what a view's
rendering is: $\mathit{render}_{W}$ and $\mathit{reads}_{W}$ are opaque.
The validator knows more. A rendering comes apart into formulas, and each
formula is read back on its own, as the part of the statement it shows.
For two views we model this. The \texttt{prose} view of a theorem is a
sentence: \emph{Let} its variables, grouped by type; \emph{Assume} its
hypotheses, each labelled when the paper refers to it; \emph{Then} its
conclusion, which may be numbered conjuncts, \emph{if and only if}, or
\emph{there is $v$ such that}. Of a definition it is \emph{We define $f$}.
What the view says is this, with each formula a value of a type $\alpha$:
\TeX{} in the extractor, and a part of a statement in the model.

\section{Attested prose}\label{sec:attested}

A sentence like \emph{preservation holds for well-typed terms} has no
formal content. The only way to tie it to Lean is to record which statement
a person checked it against, and have \verb|check| confirm that statement is
still the one under that key. The key alone will not do: it names a
\emph{declaration}, whatever the development currently says under that
name, and a theorem weakened to $\top$ keeps its name. The rendering will
not do: it changes under a renaming and not under a change of meaning
(Section~\ref{sec:bundle}). What is needed is a name for the meaning that
the paper can carry, and the statement hash is that name.

\paragraph{Two hashes.} The statement hash is of the statement's
\emph{meaning}: its type, together with every definition it depends on,
with binder names dropped and proofs left out. The signature hash is of the
statement \emph{as written}, the \texttt{lean} view. The two change
independently, and the four cases have four readings:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{l@{\quad}p{3.6cm}@{\quad}p{3.6cm}}
 & \textit{signature same} & \textit{signature changed} \\[2pt]
\textit{meaning same} & nothing changed & renamed or renotated: a warning \\[2pt]
\textit{meaning changed} & a definition it depends on changed & the statement was edited \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The lower-left case is the one a reader cannot see. The example's
substitution is a stand-in:

\begin{leandef}[sum=dLUGSgMoci, dflt=RtBzK4zyFz]{Stlc.subst}{Substitution}
\begin{LeanDisplay}
\LeanClause{M[M'] \;\deq\; M}{\leanslot{Stlc.subst}{Substitution, as a stand-in: the body is returned unchanged}}
\end{LeanDisplay}
\end{leandef}

Replace it with the real one, and no theorem about reduction changes a
character, in Lean or in the paper; their meaning does, since (Beta) uses
substitution, and their statement hashes with it (the end of this section does it). For a theorem the meaning is its proposition under the
definitions it mentions; for a definition, its own equations, so a
definition is in its own cone. A title, a group of rules or a display of
definitions, states what its members state, and is hashed as they are.

And when hashes do not collide, which is the one thing we assume of SHA-256,
one hash means one statement:

\begin{leantheorem}[sum=62mT9X28I5]{The hash names the statement}{The hash names the statement}
\label{thm:The-hash-names-the-statement}
\LeanProse{Let $\tau$ be a type, $W \in \texttt{World}(\tau)$, $S, S' \in \mathit{Stmt}_{W}$, $i, i' \in \texttt{Image}(\tau)$ and $h \in \mathit{Hash}$. Assume $\forall S : \mathit{Stmt}_{W},\ \forall S' : \mathit{Stmt}_{W},\ \mathit{hash}_{W}(S) = \mathit{hash}_{W}(S') \implies S = S'$, $\mathit{image}_{W}(S, i)$, $\mathit{image}_{W}(S', i')$, $i.\mathit{stmtHash} = h$ and $i'.\mathit{stmtHash} = h$. Then $S = S'$.}
\end{leantheorem}

\section{Rendering is faithful}
\label{sec:faithful}

The theorems say the text is the \emph{rendering} of the statement, and
that it reads back, $\mathit{reads}_{W}(r, S)$. A reader must indeed
recover the statement from the text: this is Pollack's question of
believing a machine-checked proof~\cite{Pollack98}. Wiedijk makes part of
it precise: a system is \emph{Pollack-consistent} when no contradiction
follows from equating terms it prints alike, as a consistent system is
when parsing a printed term gives back that term~\cite{Wiedijk12}.

The notation module defines both directions. Read as a grammar, each of its
rules is a mixfix production whose holes are typed by Lean's types and
grouped by precedence. A rendering is \emph{faithful} when it has exactly
one reading in that grammar, and that reading is the statement it was
rendered from, up to what the notation declares invisible: implicit
arguments, coercions, proofs, the names of hypotheses and bound variables,
and the premises and synonyms it names. That is what the world's
$\mathit{reads}$ stands for.

imprimatur checks this for each formula, by translation validation. As the
extractor renders a formula, it also builds the reading the formula should
have: an atom reads as itself, and a rule's rendering as the rule's pattern
with its holes' readings. It then parses the typeset formula, and records
in the bundle, for each view, the formulas with two readings, with none, or
with one that is not the expected reading, a \emph{misread}; \verb|check|
reports them where the formula is. This is the check Wiedijk proposes,
printing each term and parsing it back~\cite[\S8.1]{Wiedijk12}, except
that where his printer then falls back to a fail-safe syntax, imprimatur
refuses the formula. The last is what the check is for. A
renderer that left out the parentheses its holes need would print
$(\lambda r\, q.\, e) = M$ as $\lambda r\, q.\, e = M$, a formula that
reads back uniquely, and wrongly. Made to do so, imprimatur reports six
misreads in a twenty-page paper about another Lean development.

A few forms (a quantifier's binders, a case analysis, an associative chain)
are not given an expected reading, and only what is below them is
compared. There the check rests on the grammar mirroring the renderer, as
both are built from the same rules. In that twenty-page paper, 610 of the
662 formulas with one reading are compared in full, 21 in part, and 31 not
at all.

The example has a rendering that fails: its reduction relation in the
display grammar view, where both arguments are terms and the head prints as
$M \longrightarrow M$. The extractor records
\emph{variables M and M both print as M}, the block is refused, and the
theorem's $\mathit{reads}$ clause is what is withheld; the rules of
Figure~\ref{fig:example} are the view that reads back.

\paragraph{What the reader knows.} Reading back uses the types of the
formula's variables, which a reader has only if the text gives them. The
\verb|prose| view states the type of each variable it binds (\emph{Let
$b \in \mathit{Bundle}$}). A variable that a grammar or a figure leaves
free is known by its letter, as in BNF, where $p ::= \ldots$ says what $p$
ranges over. With \verb|latex_style categories := strict|, as in this
paper, imprimatur makes the convention exact: a variable whose type has a
metavariable is written with it and its own subscripts and primes, a
metavariable stands for one type, and two variables of one formula never
print alike. Each exception is an error. Appendix~\ref{app:notation} lists
the metavariables and the notation, both generated. A reader who knows
them has the grammar the formulas were checked in.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Ringer et al.\ survey proof engineering for program correctness,
including how proofs must change as the programs they are about
change~\cite{Ringer19}.

Pollack asks how a reader can believe a machine-checked proof, and
separates whether it is a derivation from whether what it derives means
what is claimed; for the second, the reader reads a printed formula, so
the parser and printer must be trusted as well as the
kernel~\cite{Pollack98}. Wiedijk makes the printer's part precise as
Pollack-consistency, shows by hidden types, coercions and notation that
HOL Light, Isabelle, Coq and Mizar fail it, and proposes printing each
term, parsing it back, and falling back to a fail-safe syntax when the two
differ~\cite{Wiedijk12}. imprimatur's printer is a paper's notation and its
reader is a person, so it parses back in a grammar that stands in for the
reader, and checks each formula rather than proving the printer; the prose
layout it proves
(Theorem~\ref{thm:The-prose-parser-undoes-the-layout}).
